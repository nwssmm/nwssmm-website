Sexually Identify Pinnipeds with Scat!

Madison Gard (mlgard00@gmail.com)

My name is Maddie Gard and I spent years of my undergraduate experience in Western Washington University’s Marine Mammal Ecology Lab before graduating in June 2023. A lot of the research projects I contributed to or led were focused on harbor seal foraging behaviors in Whatcom Creek or diets of Steller sea lions using molecular scatology. Since graduating, I published a set of qPCR primers that can be used for sexually identifying Steller and California sea lions from scat. These can be applied to a variety of scat-based pinniped studies related to marine disease, chronic stress, contaminant and biotoxin exposures, etc. to determine whether there are sex-specific responses or vulnerabilities to changing environmental conditions. So many of us are studying pinnipeds or other marine mammals, and I’d be happy to chat about the primers and how we designed them if you think they could be useful for your research!

Gard, M., Lewis, Z.K., Akmajian, A.M. et al. Comparison of paired sex ratio estimates obtained from novel molecular assays and visual surveys in the sexually dimorphic Steller sea lion (Eumetopias jubatus). Conservation Genet Resour 16, 227–235 (2024). https://doi.org/10.1007/s12686-024-01358-2

Publication

Anthropogenic and killer whale (Orcinus orca) scarring on Pacific Coast Feeding Group gray whales (Eschrichtius robustus) in northwest Washington

Liz Allyn (liz.allyn@makah.com)

Gray whales (Eschrichtius robustus) face many threats to their survival which are multi‐faceted and difficult to assess. In this study, we evaluate photographs of Pacific Coast Feeding Group (PCFG) gray whales taken between 2014 and 2020 off the northwest coast of Washington to document the occurrence of scarring from fishing gear entanglements, vessel strikes and killer whale (Orcinus orca) attacks and compare our findings with scarring prevalence in gray whales off Sakhalin Island, Russia. We found that, of the 139 PCFG whales evaluated, 11.5% had scarring from entanglements, 3.6% had scarring from vessel strikes and 25.9% had scarring from killer whale attacks. We found no differences in scarring rates between males and females. Observed rates of scarring from entanglements for PCFG whales were less than rates observed for gray whales off Sakhalin Island, Russia, while scarring rates from vessel strikes were slightly greater for PCFG whales, but the differences were not statistically significant. The frequency of scars due to killer whale attacks on PCFG whales was significantly lower than reports for whales observed at Sakhalin Island. Estimates of anthropogenic and killer whale scarring in this study are likely biased low due to limited photographic coverage of the caudal peduncle and flukes, where scarring from entanglements and killer whale attacks are most commonly observed. The methods used here were similar to studies at Sakhalin Island, suggesting the evaluations had similar biases. If we assume that observations of non‐lethal scar sources on gray whales are proportional to mortality rates from those sources, findings from this study can help evaluate if current models present plausible injury and mortality estimates for PCFG and Sakhalin Island whales given the finding of no statistical difference in non‐lethal scarring rates between the two groups.

Publication available at this link: https://journal.iwc.int/index.php/jcrm/article/view/1044

Exploring indirect effects of a classic trophic cascade between urchins and kelp on zooplankton and whales

Lisa Hildebrand (Lisa.Hildebrand@oregonstate.edu)

Kelp forest trophic cascades have been extensively researched, yet indirect effects to the zooplankton prey base and gray whales have not been explored. We investigate the correlative patterns of a trophic cascade between bull kelp and purple sea urchins on gray whales and zooplankton in Oregon, USA. Using generalized additive models (GAMs), we assess (1) temporal dynamics of the four species across 8 years, and (2) possible trophic paths from urchins to kelp, kelp as habitat to zooplankton, and kelp and zooplankton to gray whales. Temporal GAMs revealed an increase in urchin coverage, with simultaneous decline in kelp condition, zooplankton abundance and gray whale foraging time. Trophic path GAMs, which tested for correlations between species, demonstrated that urchins and kelp were negatively correlated, while kelp and zooplankton were positively correlated. Gray whales showed nuanced and site-specific correlations with zooplankton in one site, and positive correlations with kelp condition in both sites. The negative correlation between the kelp-urchin trophic cascade and zooplankton resulted in a reduced prey base for gray whales. This research provides a new perspective on the vital role kelp forests may play across multiple trophic levels and interspecies linkages.

Publication available at this link: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-59964-x

Research Blooper

Time is a construct!!!!

Lisa Hildebrand (Lisa.Hildebrand@oregonstate.edu)

I have learned to advocate for the skills that I want to learn during my PhD, rather than doing only what my advisor wants me to do. After all, that’s what graduate school is for – learning new tools that you want to add to your scientist toolbox. One of these tools is learning how to use suction-cup tags and analyze the data streams we get from them. Lo and behold, my advocating paid off – we now use suction-cup tags to better understand gray whale foraging, behavior, and risk exposure, and I have been the one to learn the ropes about how to program and prep the tags, as well as process the data. Are you starting to think, why is this in the blooper section? This very much sounds like it belongs in the small victory section! Well…for our most recent tag deployments, I was running the tag show solo. I did all the prep on my own and felt really proud of myself for doing it. Unfortunately, when we retrieved a tag that had been on a whale for just over 11 hours and I downloaded the data, I discovered an error I had made while programming the tags. These tags live in UTC time so they are 7 hours ahead of the time we live in, Pacific Standard Time (PST) during daylight savings in the summer. When you program the tags, you have to input the offset of your time (PST) to the tag time (UTC). Well rather than putting a -7, I input a +7, programming the tag to be Indochina Time (ICT). Because we program our tag’s camera to only turn on during daylight hours (6 am to 9 pm), it meant that we didn’t record video for the majority of our 11+ hour deployment because the tag was happily sleeping thinking it was ICT nighttime…Better luck next time I guess.
